    Before you arrest
      Necessity / procedural requirements
      At the point of arrest — legally required disclosures
      Under Code G, at the time of arrest (or as soon as practicable after) you must make clear to the person: the fact that they are under arrest, and the grounds and reason for the arrest, even if these seem obvious. Failure to do this may make the arrest unlawful.
        On arrival at service custody — entitlements to explain
          Time limits and reviews
          Section 98 AFA 2006: a person arrested under section 67 must not be kept in service custody without charge except in accordance with the further provisions of the Act. Confirm current review periods and authorisation levels with your custody officer/SOPs.

              Sexual offences
              Sexual Offences Act 2003 (SOA 2003): consent and the presumptions under sections 75 and 76. Reference and training aid only. Always check the current Act and SPCoP.
              Consent (s.74)
              NEEDS VERIFICATION

              In outline, a person consents if they agree by choice and have the freedom and capacity to make that choice.

              Confirm the exact wording of s.74 against legislation.gov.uk before relying on it.

              Offences covered (ss.1 to 4)
              VERIFIED 14 NOV 2026
              • Section 1: Rape
              • Section 2: Assault by penetration
              • Section 3: Sexual assault
              • Section 4: Causing a person to engage in sexual activity without consent

              The s.75 presumptions apply to these four offences. The "relevant act" is defined in s.77.

              s.75 Rebuttable presumptions
              REBUTTABLEVERIFIED 14 NOV 2026

              What the prosecution must prove

              1. The defendant did the relevant act.
              2. One of the circumstances below existed.
              3. The defendant knew that circumstance existed.

              Effect

              The complainant is taken not to have consented, and the defendant is taken not to have reasonably believed they consented, unless sufficient evidence is adduced to raise an issue on that point. That is why it is rebuttable.

              Circumstances (s.75(2))

              (a) Violence, or fear of immediate violence

              Any person was using violence against the complainant, or causing them to fear that immediate violence would be used against them, at or immediately before the relevant act.

              (b) Fear of violence to another person

              Any person was causing the complainant to fear that violence was being used, or that immediate violence would be used, against another person, at or immediately before the relevant act.

              (c) Unlawful detention

              The complainant was, and the defendant was not, unlawfully detained at the time of the relevant act.

              (d) Asleep or unconscious

              The complainant was asleep or otherwise unconscious at the time of the relevant act.

              (e) Disability prevents communication

              Because of the complainant's physical disability, they would not have been able at the time to communicate to the defendant whether they consented.

              (f) Stupefying substance

              Any person had administered to the complainant, or caused them to take, without their consent, a substance capable of causing or enabling them to be stupefied or overpowered at the time of the relevant act.

              For (a) and (b), where the relevant act is part of a continuous series of sexual activities, "immediately before" refers to the point just before the first sexual activity in that series began (s.75(3)).

              Case law
              Case example

              R v Ciccarelli [2011] EWCA Crim 2665 (complainant asleep, s.75(2)(d)): s.75 is an evidential provision and does not reverse the burden of proof. Where the complainant was asleep, a claimed belief in consent must rest on some evidence beyond the fanciful or speculative, or reasonable belief does not go to the jury. A single earlier advance by the complainant while awake elsewhere, with no attempt to wake her, was not enough.

              Read the judgment · CHECKED 14 NOV 2026

              s.76 Irrebuttable presumptions
              IRREBUTTABLEVERIFIED 14 NOV 2026

              The rule

              If it is proved that the defendant did the relevant act and one of the circumstances below existed, it is conclusively presumed that:

              • (a) the complainant did not consent to the relevant act, and
              • (b) the defendant did not believe that the complainant consented.

              The defence cannot challenge the presumption.

              Circumstances (s.76(2))

              (a) Deception about nature or purpose

              The defendant intentionally deceived the complainant as to the nature or purpose of the relevant act.

              (b) Impersonation

              The defendant intentionally induced the complainant to consent to the relevant act by impersonating a person known personally to the complainant.

              Examples given in the Explanatory Notes: falsely claiming that digital penetration is medically necessary; impersonating the complainant's partner.

              NEEDS VERIFICATION Confirm which offences s.76 applies to. It is understood to be the same four offences as s.75 (ss.1 to 4).

              Case law
              Case examples

              R v Devonald [2008] EWCA Crim 527: s.76(2)(a) was held capable of applying. The complainant was deceived about the purpose of the act, and the court noted that the provision applies rarely.

              R v Jheeta [2007] EWCA Crim 1699: s.76 did not apply, because the deception concerned the complainant's circumstances, not the nature or purpose of intercourse. The conviction stood under s.74, because the complainant's agreement was not a free choice.

              R v McNally [2013] EWCA Crim 1051 (deception as to gender): s.76 was not engaged, so the question was decided under s.74. Depending on the circumstances, deception as to gender can vitiate consent.

              Devonald · Jheeta · McNally · CHECKED 14 NOV 2026
